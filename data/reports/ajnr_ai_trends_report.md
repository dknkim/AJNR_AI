# AI in Neuroradiology: A Bibliometric Analysis of Research in the *American Journal of Neuroradiology* (2017–2026)

---

## 1. Executive Summary

The landscape of artificial intelligence (AI) research in the *American Journal of Neuroradiology* (AJNR) over the 10-year period from 2017 to 2026 reflects a transformative evolution—from early exploratory studies to a mature, high-impact field with clear clinical and methodological direction. The corpus comprises **229 papers**, generating **5,456 total citations** and an age-adjusted citation count of **912.0**, indicating a substantial and sustained scholarly influence. The average paper garners **23.8 citations**, with a notable concentration of high-impact work in the mid-to-late 2010s and early 2020s.

The trajectory of AI publishing in AJNR reveals a **sharp acceleration in output**, particularly from 2021 onward. While only 6 papers were published in 2017, the number surged to **55 in 2025**, marking a decade of exponential growth. This growth was not merely quantitative: **2018 stands as the peak year for citation impact**, with a total of 1,321 citations and an age-adjusted total of 146.8—far exceeding any other year. This spike was driven by landmark reviews and foundational studies that set the stage for the field. The 2021–2025 period saw a shift toward **clinical validation, multimodal integration, and real-world implementation**, with a growing emphasis on **prognostic modeling, acute stroke applications, and large language models (LLMs)**.

The dominant subfields are **Detection / classification (CADx)**, **Image segmentation**, **Quantitative imaging**, and **Prognosis / outcome prediction**, each contributing over 85 papers. These areas reflect a clinical focus on diagnosis, anatomical quantification, and patient outcome prediction. Among the fastest-growing subfields are **Large language models / NLP** (CAGR: 4.6%) and **Prognosis / outcome prediction** (CAGR: 20.9%), signaling a shift toward AI systems that interpret complex clinical narratives and predict long-term outcomes. Conversely, **Machine learning (general)** and **transfer learning** have declined, suggesting a move from generic algorithms to specialized, task-specific models.

Headline emerging methods include **convolutional neural networks (CNNs)**, **radiomics**, and **U-Net architectures**, with **CNNs** being the most cited method family (131 papers, 3,628 citations). High-impact papers frequently leverage **external validation**, **multi-center datasets**, and **public benchmarks** like **The Cancer Imaging Archive (TCIA)** and **ADNI**. The most cited paper—*“Deep-learning convolutional neural networks accurately classify genetic mutations in gliomas”* (2018, 437 citations)—exemplifies this trend: it combined **deep learning**, **genetic prediction**, **image segmentation**, and **explainability**, and was validated on a multi-institutional cohort.

The most influential authors are **Zaharchuk G.** (18 papers, 1,085 citations), **Filippi C.G.** (6 papers, 968 citations, mean 161.3), and **Wintermark M.** (12 papers, 929 citations, mean 77.4). Stanford University leads in both volume (23 papers) and impact (1,382 citations), while **Mayo Clinic** and **Harvard Medical School** also feature prominently. However, **Stanford University** and **Stanford University School of Medicine** stand out for their **high mean citation per paper (60.1 and 74.3)**, indicating a focus on high-impact, often multi-institutional or clinically validated work.

Methodological rigor has improved over time, with **external validation** rising from 0% in 2017 to **29.4% in 2024**, and **multi-center data** usage peaking at 36.7% in 2021. However, **open code availability remains negligible** (only 3.6% in 2025), and **open data sharing is still rare**, suggesting a gap in reproducibility and open science. The field is maturing, but transparency lags behind innovation.

## 3. Subfield Growth and Decline

- **Fastest-growing subfields**:
  - **Prognosis / outcome prediction** (CAGR: 20.9%) — driven by radiomics, deep learning, and multi-center validation.
  - **Large language models / NLP** (CAGR: 4.6%) — surged in 2021–2025, reflecting integration into clinical workflows and reporting.
  - **Explainability / trustworthy AI** — grew from 8 to 26 papers, indicating rising demand for model transparency.

- **Stable or moderate growth**:
  - **Detection / classification (CADx)** — remains dominant (158 papers), with steady growth (CAGR: 16.0%).
  - **Image segmentation** — 132 papers, CAGR: 11.2%.
  - **Quantitative imaging** — 149 papers, CAGR: 13.7%.

- **Declining or stagnant subfields**:
  - **Machine learning (general)** — declined from 1 to 0 papers (CAGR: -100%).
  - **Transfer learning** — declined from 1 to 0.
  - **Multimodal AI** — grew from 3 to 9, but remains low-volume.
  - **Image reconstruction / acceleration** — saw a decline in recent years (from 41 to 2 papers in 2026), despite early momentum.

- **Emerging niche subfields**:
  - **Federated / privacy-preserving learning** — 5 papers (all in 2025–2026), signaling privacy-aware AI development.
  - **AI agents / autonomous systems** — 1 paper (2023), indicating early exploration of AI-driven clinical decision support.
